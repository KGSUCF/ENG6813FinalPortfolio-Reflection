Syllabus & Course Map

How the semester runs

Fall 2026, in person, Tuesday/Thursday. Tuesdays introduce concepts; Thursdays are active-learning workshops where students practice the method before submitting anything individually.

Delivery model

Tuesday meetings introduce concepts, readings, and guided analysis. Thursday meetings function as workshops where students practice course methods collaboratively before completing individual submissions.

Zoom recordings and Canvas discussion support access, review, and peer engagement, but the course is designed as an in-person class.

Materials and tools

No paid AI subscription is required. Students use freely available or institutionally available tools: Canvas, web browsers, library resources, Voyant or a comparable text-analysis tool, search engines, reverse image/source tracing tools, and a chatbot such as Claude or another approved tool.

All substantial projects must use public-domain, openly licensed, government-produced, instructor-approved, or otherwise legally usable materials.

Week Unit / Focus Reading for the Week Tuesday Class Thursday Workshop Assignment Due
1 Unit 1: Persuasion vs. manipulation Lloyd Bitzer, “The Rhetorical Situation” — excerpt Introduce course questions: What is persuasion? When does persuasion become manipulation? What does a message want its audience to believe, feel, or do? Compare two short public-domain or openly available messages on the same topic. Identify audience, purpose, and desired response. Short reflection: “When does persuasion become manipulation?”
2 Audience, purpose, and framing Lisa Ede and Andrea Lunsford, “Audience Addressed/Audience Invoked” — excerpt Discuss audience as more than “who sees the message.” Rewrite the same neutral message for three different audiences and compare tone, examples, emphasis, and implied values. Audience analysis worksheet on a short public-domain speech, ad, or public message.
3 Emotional appeals, authority, repetition, loaded language, and visual persuasion John Berger, Ways of Seeing — chapter/episode on publicity and advertising Discuss visual persuasion, desire, aspiration, identity, and advertising as a system of meaning. Annotate a persuasive text or image for emotional appeals, authority, repetition, loaded words, and visual framing. Close-reading annotation: identify five persuasive moves and explain how they work.
4 Fallacies, omissions, weak reasoning, and fair persuasion Melanie Gagich, Emilie Zickel, and Terri Pantuso, “3.6 Logical Fallacies” Distinguish reasonable claims, fair persuasion, unsupported claims, weak reasoning, omissions, and fallacies. Play Point Taken as an instructor-created fallacy-identification game. Then complete the Rhetorical Design Lab, creating a short persuasive message that includes both reasonable claims and flawed reasoning. Student-authored rhetorical plan, short persuasive message, and reflection on what peers noticed.
5 Unit 2: First reactions, bias, and assumptions Short reading on confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, or cognitive bias Discuss why people can misread information even when they are intelligent and well-intentioned. Record first reaction to a message, then reread slowly and compare first impressions with later analysis. Reflection: “What did I notice only after slowing down?”
6 Identity, belonging, outrage, and “people like us” Accessible excerpt or secondary reading on Kenneth Burke’s concept of identification Discuss how persuasion creates belonging, division, shared identity, and implied enemies. Analyze how a message creates “people like us” and “people like them.” Short analysis: identify audience identity, implied values, and emotional pressure.
7 Evidence, claims, and missing context Sam Wineburg / Sarah McGrew on lateral reading, or Stanford/Digital Inquiry Group Civic Online Reasoning materials Introduce lateral reading, source evaluation, and the difference between a claim, evidence, interpretation, and assumption. Sort statements into fact, interpretation, opinion, unsupported claim, weak inference, and fallacy. Evidence-check mini-assignment: verify or complicate one claim from a public-domain or openly available message.
8 Images, numbers, graphs, and selective evidence Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West, Calling Bullshit — excerpt, or an open data-literacy reading Discuss how numbers, graphs, images, screenshots, quotes, and statistics can mislead when context is missing. Inspect a graph, image, quote, statistic, or screenshot and identify what is shown, hidden, cropped, exaggerated, or missing. See the 1% tax visualization. “Missing Context” worksheet. Students also submit an initial idea for the signature assignment topic or artifact.
9 Unit 3: Platforms, algorithms, search, and attention Safiya Umoja Noble, Algorithms of Oppression — excerpt, or Siva Vaidhyanathan excerpt Discuss how platforms, search engines, recommendation systems, advertising systems, and algorithms shape what people see and believe. Compare search results, headlines, recommendation paths, or platform interfaces. Short platform reflection: “What did the platform make easy to see, and what did it make harder to question?”
10 Digital humanities pattern reading Stéfan Sinclair and Geoffrey Rockwell, “Teaching Computer-Assisted Text Analysis” Introduce beginner-friendly digital humanities text analysis and what pattern-reading tools can and cannot explain. Use Voyant or a comparable text-analysis tool on a short public-domain text or instructor-provided corpus. Tool reflection: What did the tool reveal that close reading did not? What did it fail to explain?
11 Chatbots as interpretive assistants Ethan and Lilach Mollick, “Assigning AI: Seven Approaches for Students, with Prompts” — excerpt Discuss AI as a tool, not an authority. Introduce focused prompting for audience, purpose, persuasion, missing context, and evidence questions. Analyze a persuasive message manually, then ask a chatbot a focused interpretive question and compare results. AI comparison memo: “What did I see? What did AI see? What did AI miss, flatten, or overstate?”
12 AI output, verification, and responsible use Mills, Ruiz, Lee, Coenraad, Fusco, Roschelle, and Weisgrau, “AI Literacy: A Framework to Understand, Evaluate, and Use Emerging Technology” Discuss AI literacy, verification, bias, hallucination, documentation, and ethical use of AI in academic work. Examine an AI-generated summary, explanation, or analysis and compare it with the original source. AI-use note practice: prompts used, useful output, errors or omissions, and verification steps.
13 Unit 4: Preparing the signature assignment Revisit selected readings from Bitzer, Ede/Lunsford, Wineburg/McGrew, and Mollick/Mollick Review major course methods: close reading, audience analysis, evidence-checking, tool-assisted analysis, AI reflection, and public communication. Proposal and preliminary reading workshop. Students present their chosen artifact or small information environment, preliminary close reading, audience/purpose analysis, likely persuasive techniques, evidence questions, and planned AI/tool use. Peers help test whether the project is manageable, rich enough, and ready for further analysis. Signature Assignment Proposal and Preliminary Reading: artifact, rationale, audience, preliminary interpretation, likely persuasive techniques, possible evidence questions, and planned tools.
14 Communicating responsible knowledge Short public humanities / digital humanities reading, or examples of effective public-facing guides Discuss how to translate analysis into clear public guidance without oversimplifying the issue. Draft workshop. Students bring a draft packet: close-reading section, evidence-check section or plan, AI/tool-use note draft, and public-facing guide outline or prototype. Peers give feedback on clarity, usefulness, accessibility, evidence, audience, and overclaiming. Draft Checkpoint and Peer Review Reflection: close-reading section, AI/tool-use plan or note, public-facing guide outline/prototype, and brief response to peer feedback.
Final Exam Period Signature assignment submission No new reading No final exam. Optional conferences or presentations if scheduled. Students complete and submit the final project. Signature Assignment Due: “Reading Persuasion in the Wild: A Public Guide to Responsible Interpretation,” including analytical case study, public-facing guide, AI/tool documentation, reflection, and revision note.

Why 400 points for one project

The signature assignment is staged across three graded checkpoints — proposal (100), draft (100), final (200) — so students are rewarded for process and revision, not just the last thing they hand in.
