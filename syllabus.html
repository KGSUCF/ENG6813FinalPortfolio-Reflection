Syllabus & Course Map

How the semester runs

Fall 2026, in person, Tuesday/Thursday. Tuesdays introduce concepts; Thursdays are active-learning workshops where students practice the method before submitting anything individually.

Delivery model

Tuesday meetings introduce concepts, readings, and guided analysis. Thursday meetings function as workshops where students practice course methods collaboratively before completing individual submissions.

Zoom recordings and Canvas discussion support access, review, and peer engagement, but the course is designed as an in-person class.

Materials and tools

No paid AI subscription is required. Students use freely available or institutionally available tools: Canvas, web browsers, library resources, Voyant or a comparable text-analysis tool, search engines, reverse image/source tracing tools, and a chatbot such as Claude or another approved tool.

All substantial projects must use public-domain, openly licensed, government-produced, instructor-approved, or otherwise legally usable materials.

On the readings: openly posted copies of scholarly work have a habit of disappearing. Every reading below is therefore linked through the UCF Library or the creator’s own site, whichever is more likely to still be there next year. Links marked UCF Library require a UCF login; Open access links are readable by anyone.

Fall 2026 Tuesday/Thursday in-person model — 14 weeks plus the final exam period.
Week Unit / Focus Reading for the Week Tuesday Class Thursday Workshop Assignment Due
1 Unit 1: Rhetoric and Persuasion — persuasion vs. manipulation Lloyd Bitzer, “The Rhetorical Situation” — excerpt on exigence, audience, and constraints UCF Library Introduce the course’s central questions: What is persuasion? When does persuasion become manipulation? What does a message want its audience to believe, feel, or do? Compare two short public-domain or openly available messages on the same topic. Identify audience, purpose, and desired response. Short reflection: “When does persuasion become manipulation?”
2 Audience, purpose, and framing Lisa Ede and Andrea Lunsford, “Audience Addressed/Audience Invoked” — excerpt UCF Library Discuss audience as more than “who sees the message.” Introduce the idea that messages imagine, target, flatter, pressure, or exclude audiences. Rewrite the same neutral message for three different audiences. Compare how tone, examples, emphasis, and implied values change. Audience analysis worksheet on a short public-domain speech, ad, or public message.
3 Emotional appeals, authority, repetition, loaded language, and visual persuasion John Berger, Ways of Seeing — chapter/episode on publicity and advertising UCF Library Discuss visual persuasion, desire, aspiration, identity, and advertising as a system of meaning. In groups, annotate a persuasive text or image for emotional appeals, authority, repetition, loaded words, and visual framing. Close-reading annotation: identify five persuasive moves and explain how they work.
4 Fallacies, omissions, weak reasoning, and fair persuasion Melanie Gagich, Emilie Zickel, and Terri Pantuso, “Logical Fallacies” Open access Open with Point Taken as an instructor-created fallacy-identification game. Distinguish reasonable claims, fair persuasion, unsupported claims, weak reasoning, omissions, and logical fallacies. Rhetorical Design Lab: students create a short persuasive message that includes both reasonable claims and flawed reasoning. Peers identify the techniques. Student-authored rhetorical plan, short persuasive message, and reflection on what peers noticed.
5 Unit 2: Human Vulnerability to Misinformation — first reactions, bias, and assumptions Neurolaunch, Navigating Logical Fallacies and Cognitive Biases Open access Discuss why people can misread information even when they are intelligent and well-intentioned. Students privately record their first reaction to a message, then reread it slowly and compare first impressions with later analysis. Reflection: “What did I notice only after slowing down?”
6 Identity, belonging, outrage, and “people like us” Brooke L. Quigley, “Identification” as a Key Term in Kenneth Burke’s Rhetorical Theory Open access Discuss how persuasion creates belonging, division, shared identity, and implied enemies. Analyze how a message creates “people like us” and “people like them.” Identify implied values and emotional pressure. Short analysis: identify audience identity, implied values, and emotional pressure.
7 Evidence, claims, and missing context Sam Wineburg / Sarah McGrew on lateral reading UCF Library Introduce lateral reading, source evaluation, and the difference between a claim, evidence, interpretation, and assumption. Sort statements into fact, interpretation, opinion, unsupported claim, weak inference, and fallacy. Evidence-check mini-assignment: verify or complicate one claim from a public-domain or openly available message.
8 Images, numbers, graphs, and selective evidence Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West, Calling Bullshit — excerpt UCF Library Discuss how numbers, graphs, images, screenshots, quotes, and statistics can mislead when context is missing. Inspect a graph, image, quote, statistic, or screenshot and identify what is shown, hidden, cropped, exaggerated, or missing. See the 1% tax visualization. “Missing Context” worksheet: what is shown, what is hidden, and what else would we need to know? Students also submit an initial idea for the signature assignment topic or artifact.
9 Unit 3: Technology-Based Persuasion — platforms, algorithms, search, and attention Safiya Umoja Noble, Algorithms of Oppression — excerpt UCF Library Discuss how platforms, search engines, recommendation systems, advertising systems, and algorithms shape what people see and believe. Compare search results, headlines, recommendation paths, or platform interfaces. Identify what the platform makes easy to see and what it makes harder to question. Short platform reflection: “What did the platform make easy to see, and what did it make harder to question?”
10 Digital humanities pattern reading Stéfan Sinclair and Geoffrey Rockwell, “Teaching Computer-Assisted Text Analysis” UCF Library Introduce beginner-friendly digital humanities text analysis. Discuss what pattern-reading tools can reveal and what they cannot explain. Use Voyant or a comparable text-analysis tool on a short public-domain text or instructor-provided corpus. Tool reflection: What did the tool reveal that close reading did not? What did it fail to explain?
11 Chatbots as interpretive assistants Ethan and Lilach Mollick, “Assigning AI: Seven Approaches for Students, with Prompts” — excerpt UCF Library Discuss AI as a tool, not an authority. Introduce focused prompting for audience, purpose, persuasion, missing context, and evidence questions. Students analyze a persuasive message themselves first, then ask a chatbot a focused interpretive question and compare the results. AI comparison memo: “What did I see? What did AI see? What did AI miss, flatten, or overstate?”
12 AI output, verification, and responsible use Mills, Ruiz, Lee, Coenraad, Fusco, Roschelle, and Weisgrau, “AI Literacy: A Framework to Understand, Evaluate, and Use Emerging Technology” Open access Discuss AI literacy, verification, bias, hallucination, documentation, and the ethical use of AI in academic work. Examine an AI-generated summary, explanation, or analysis and compare it with the original source. Practice documenting prompts, outputs, errors, and verification steps. AI-use note practice: prompts used, useful output, errors or omissions, and verification steps.
13 Unit 4: Better Learning and Better Judgment — preparing the signature assignment Revisit selected readings from Bitzer, Ede/Lunsford, Wineburg/McGrew, and Mollick/Mollick Review the major course methods: close reading, audience analysis, evidence-checking, tool-assisted analysis, AI reflection, and public communication. Proposal and preliminary reading workshop. Students present their chosen public-domain or openly usable artifact or small information environment, preliminary close reading, audience/purpose analysis, likely persuasive techniques, evidence questions, and planned AI/tool use. Peers help test whether the project is manageable, rich enough, and ready for further analysis. Signature Assignment Proposal and Preliminary Reading: artifact, rationale, audience, preliminary interpretation, likely persuasive techniques, possible evidence questions, and planned tools.
14 Communicating responsible knowledge (shifted to Dec 1–3 due to Thanksgiving break, Nov 23–27) Short public humanities / digital humanities reading, or examples of effective public-facing guides Discuss how to translate analysis into clear public guidance without oversimplifying the issue. Draft workshop. Students bring a draft packet: close-reading section, evidence-check section or plan, AI/tool-use note draft, and public-facing guide outline or prototype. Peers give feedback on clarity, usefulness, accessibility, evidence, audience, and overclaiming. Draft Checkpoint and Peer Review Reflection: close-reading section, AI/tool-use plan or note, public-facing guide outline/prototype, and brief response to peer feedback.
Final Exam Period Signature assignment submission No new reading No final exam. Optional conferences or presentations if scheduled. Students complete and submit the final project. Students must include a short revision note explaining what peer feedback they used, rejected, or reconsidered. Signature Assignment Due: “Reading Persuasion in the Wild: A Public Guide to Responsible Interpretation,” including analytical case study, public-facing guide, AI/tool documentation, reflection, and revision note.

Why 400 points for one project

The signature assignment is staged across three graded checkpoints — proposal (100), draft (100), final (200) — so students are rewarded for process and revision, not just the last thing they hand in.
