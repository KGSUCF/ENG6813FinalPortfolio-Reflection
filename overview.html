Course Overview

Why this course

An introductory course about meaning-making, persuasion, misinformation, and judgment in an AI-mediated world.

Students need more than traditional “media literacy” in the narrow sense. They need practical, humanistic tools for reading persuasive messages, evaluating evidence, recognizing misleading uses of images and numbers, and understanding the role of technology in shaping belief.

Media Literacy in the Humanities examines the stories, emotions, platforms, habits, and assumptions that influence what people notice, trust, question, and share. Students also consider how their own attention, biases, identities, and interpretive habits affect what they believe.

The course does not assume technical expertise or require prerequisites. It invites students to slow down, ask better questions, and practice responsible interpretation.

A humanities course, not a journalism course

Media literacy here is not a specialized technical skill. It is part of modern humanistic education: learning to read, question, interpret, and act responsibly in a world full of persuasive messages.

Four units

The sequence moves from how persuasion works, to why people are susceptible, to how technology amplifies both, to what better judgment looks like in practice.

I

Rhetoric and Persuasion

Emotional appeals, framing, authority, repetition, audience, fallacies, omissions, weak reasoning, and fair persuasion.

II

Human Vulnerability to Misinformation

Cognitive bias, identity, outrage, first reactions, belonging, and the desire for simple stories.

III

Technology-Based Persuasion

Social media platforms, algorithms, advertising systems, search engines, generative AI, and digital humanities tools.

IV

Better Learning and Better Judgment

Source evaluation, lateral reading, data context, fact-checking habits, ethical AI use, reflective skepticism, and public-facing communication.
